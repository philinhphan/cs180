1. Overview

Between 1909 and 1915 Sergei Mikhailovich Prokudin-Gorskii photographed the Russian Empire in colour by exposing three glass negatives of every scene through blue, green and red filters. The Library of Congress (LoC) scanned the surviving plates, each as one tall grayscale image with the three exposures stacked top to bottom (B, G, R). The three exposures were taken a few seconds apart and the plates were not perfectly registered in the camera, so simply stacking the thirds as colour channels produces the fringed mess in the middle image above. The task of this project is to align the plates automatically and produce a clean colour photograph, using nothing more than a translation model and image-matching metrics implemented from scratch.

What I built, in the order the write-up follows:

  • a single-scale exhaustive search over a window of displacements, scored with the L2 (Euclidean) distance and with normalized cross-correlation (NCC) on raw pixel values;
  • a coarse-to-fine image pyramid (my own binomial blur + 2× subsampling) that makes the same search feasible on the 3700 × 3200 px, 16-bit TIFF plates in about 1.5 seconds each;
  • an evaluation on the 14 provided plates plus 4 full-resolution plates I downloaded from the LoC. With raw-pixel NCC every plate except emir aligns to the pixel; with L2 two plates fail;
  • bells and whistles: gradient-magnitude features (which fix emir), automatic cropping, automatic contrast, automatic white balance, a colour-mapping experiment against the LoC composites, rotation and scale refinement (the plates turn out to differ in scale by ≈0.4 %), and the same pipeline applied to three photographic plates from the Palomar sky survey.

Loading. I read each scan with Pillow and convert it to float32 in [0, 1] by dividing by the maximum of the integer type: 255 for the JPEGs, 65535 for the TIFFs. (The data-directory skeleton divides by 255 unconditionally, which would map a 16-bit scan to [0, 257].) Working in floating point on the same scale is what makes L2 values and NCC values comparable across images and pyramid levels.

Splitting. The plate height is divided by three and floored, and the scan is cut into the top (B), middle (G) and bottom (R) thirds; the one or two leftover rows are dropped so that the three channels have identical shapes.

Displacement convention. All displacements on this page are the shift (x, y) applied to the moving plate (G or R) so that it lands on the reference plate (B): positive x moves content to the right, positive y moves it down. In code this is exactly np.roll(channel, (y, x), axis=(0, 1)). The final colour image is np.dstack([roll(R, d_R), roll(G, d_G), B]).

Ignoring borders. Every plate carries a saturated white scanner margin and a black film edge, and np.roll wraps the pixels that leave one side back in on the other. Both would dominate any pixel-wise score, so a metric is only ever evaluated on the interior of the two images: I drop 15 % of the rows and columns on every side (plus, at every pyramid level, at least the largest displacement the search can produce, so the wrapped band can never enter the scored region). Fifteen percent is generous — the measured margins reach 8 % and the largest shift 5.5 % of the plate — and still leaves 70 % × 70 % of the frame, about 6 million pixels, for scoring a full-size plate.

3. Single-scale exhaustive search (L2 and NCC)

The naive method is exactly what the assignment describes: for every integer displacement (dx, dy) in a window — [−15, 15] in both directions, 961 candidates — shift the moving channel, compare its interior with the interior of the reference, and keep the best score.

best = None
for dy in range(-15, 16):
    for dx in range(-15, 16):
        moved = interior(np.roll(mov, (dy, dx)))     # implemented as a slice, see below
        cost  = metric(interior(ref), moved)          # L2 distance, or -NCC
        if best is None or (cost, |dx|+|dy|) < best:  # exact ties -> smallest shift
            best = (cost, |dx|+|dy|), (dx, dy)

Two implementation details matter for speed. Since the interior margin is always larger than the displacement, the interior of the rolled image is just a slice of the original, mov[my−dy : h−my−dy, mx−dx : w−mx−dx], so no copy is made per candidate (a unit test checks that this equals roll-then-crop). And the reference interior is normalised once per search, so each candidate costs one subtraction and one or two dot products over the interior.

The two metrics

L2 / Euclidean distance. The square root of the summed squared pixel differences; I report the root mean square, which has the same minimiser but stays comparable between pyramid levels of different size.

L2(A, B) = √( Σi (Ai − Bi)² )    (minimised)

L2 rewards two images for having the same brightness at every pixel. That is a problem here: the three plates were exposed through different filters, so the same object has different intensities in B, G and R — a red robe is bright in the red plate and dark in the blue one. L2 will happily trade structural alignment for a displacement that makes the average brightness match better.

Normalized cross-correlation. Subtract each image's mean, divide by its norm, and take the dot product of the two resulting unit vectors:

NCC(A, B) = ⟨ (A − μA) / ‖A − μA‖ , (B − μB) / ‖B − μB‖ ⟩ ∈ [−1, 1]    (maximised; I minimise −NCC)

Because of the mean subtraction and the normalisation, NCC is invariant to any change B → αB + β of one channel's brightness and contrast, which is exactly the difference between two plates of the same scene taken through different filters. (The mean-subtracted, normalised L2 distance is 2 − 2·NCC, so a zero-normalised L2 would give identical results; the plain L2 the assignment asks for does not.) NCC is what I use as the default metric; L2 results are always reported alongside.

4. Coarse-to-fine pyramid alignment

The exhaustive search does not scale to the full-size plates. The red plate of melons is displaced by 177 px, so the window would have to be at least [−180, 180]² — about 130 000 candidates, each scored on 6 million interior pixels: on the order of 1012 operations per channel, tens of minutes per image in NumPy. An image pyramid solves this by finding the displacement on a tiny version of the image first, where 177 px is only 11 px, and then refining it level by level.

Building the pyramid

I implemented the pyramid myself. Each level is produced from the previous one by

  1. a separable blur with the 5-tap binomial kernel [1 4 6 4 1] / 16, applied along rows and then columns as weighted sums of shifted slices (pure NumPy, reflect-padded so the image mean is kept). The kernel is a close approximation of a Gaussian with σ ≈ 1 px. This is the anti-aliasing step: without it, subsampling folds fine texture into false low-frequency structure that misleads the coarse search;
  2. keeping every second row and column, so a level of size h × w becomes ⌊h/2⌋ × ⌊w/2⌋.

The number of levels is chosen from the image size so that the coarsest level is at least 150 px on its short side: ⌊log₂(min(h, w) / 150)⌋ halvings, which is 4 for the TIFF plates (coarsest level 235 × 202 px) and 1 for the small JPEGs. A unit test checks that my downsampler agrees with scikit-image's anti-aliased rescale (correlation > 0.99) while staying entirely home-made.

The coarse-to-fine search

The search is the single-scale routine from Section 3, run once per level from the coarsest to the finest:

  1. Coarsest level: exhaustive search around (0, 0) with radius 19. Because a displacement of d pixels at full resolution is only d / 24 at level 4, this window covers ±304 px at full resolution — comfortably more than the largest displacement in the collection (about 180 px). (The radius is 15 by default and is widened automatically so that the window always covers 8 % of the image size.)
  2. Every finer level: double the estimate from the level above and search a small window around it. Doubling an integer estimate is off by at most about one pixel, plus the half-pixel phase of the subsampling, so a radius of 3 is sufficient once a level has succeeded; I use a shrinking schedule (19 → 9 → 4 → 3 → 3) to tolerate a slightly wrong coarse estimate at almost no cost.

The reference channel's pyramid is built once and reused for both G and R. The whole procedure is equivalent to the recursive formulation suggested in the assignment (align the half-size images, then refine), written as a loop so that every level's estimate can be logged; here is the trace for the melons red plate:

5. Extra plates from the Library of Congress

I downloaded 4 additional full-resolution negatives from the LoC. The web pages block scripted clients, but the collection's JSON API (https://www.loc.gov/collections/prokudin-gorskii/?fo=json) returns for every item the id of its prok resource, and the master scan is served at https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/pnp/prok/NNN00/NNNNNu.tif (8-bit, ≈36 MB). main.py download fetches them and writes a credits file. I picked plates that stress different aspects of the method:

  • isfandiyar — Isfandiyar, Khan of the Russian protectorate of Khorezm (Khiva), three-quarter length portrait, in official robes, seated on chair, outdoors (1910). A portrait in saturated robes, like the Emir of Bukhara: the three plates have very different brightness, which stresses intensity-based metrics. LoC item, digital id prok.00151.
  • karlinskii — Pinkhus Karlinskii, 84 years old, 66 years of service. Supervisor of the Chernigov floodgate (1909). A standing portrait with water and sky behind it: large smooth regions with little texture. LoC item, digital id prok.00882.
  • peasant_girls — Peasant girls [Russian Empire] (1909). Three girls in brightly patterned clothes: fine texture and strong colour differences between plates. LoC item, digital id prok.01043.
  • vitebsk_cathedral — Vitebsk. Uspenski (Assumption) cathedral (1912). Architecture with straight edges and a large sky: a landscape-type scene to complement the portraits. LoC item, digital id prok.00428.

All four align with the same fixed parameters; the two metrics agree to within two pixels. karlinskii is interesting because half of the frame is moving water, which no longer matches between exposures — the search still locks onto the static parts of the scene.

6. Failure analysis

Emir of Bukhara with raw pixel metrics

Both metrics misalign the red plate of emir: NCC gives R = (44, 64) and L2 gives R = (44, 66), while the correct displacement is (40, 107). (The green plate, G = (24, 49), is fine.) The reason is the one the assignment hints at: the emir's robe is bright blue with dark patterns, so it is bright in the blue plate and dark in the red one, while his face, the wooden doors and the floor are the opposite. The relation between the two plates' intensities is not a single brightness change but flips sign from region to region. L2 cannot cope with that at all, and even NCC's affine invariance is not enough: the anti-correlated robe pulls the correlation down at the correct displacement, and a wrong displacement wins. The trace shows the problem starts at the coarsest level, where the best NCC is only about 0.28, and every finer level then refines a wrong answer.

The second failure is specific to L2. On church the L2 search for the red plate runs to the +x edge of the window at every level — the RMSE keeps decreasing the further the red plate is pushed to the right — and ends at R = (401, 78), hundreds of pixels off. The scene is a bright sky over dark water; sliding the red plate sideways shifts its bright and dark areas over the reference in a way that lowers the absolute intensity difference without any structure lining up. NCC, which only cares about the correlation of the deviations from the mean, finds (−4, 58) without difficulty.

7. Bells and whistles

7.1 Better features: gradient magnitude

Motivation. The failure on emir is a failure of intensity as a feature. Edges, on the other hand, are in the same place in all three plates whatever their brightness or the sign of their contrast.

Method. Before scoring, both channels are replaced by their Sobel gradient magnitude: the image is smoothed with the same binomial kernel (σ ≈ 1 px, to suppress film grain), filtered with Gx = [1 2 1]ᵀ/4 · [−1 0 1]/2 and its transpose (written as shifted-slice arithmetic), and √(gx² + gy²) is clipped at its 99th percentile and scaled to [0, 1], so a few very strong edges (plate borders, specular highlights) cannot dominate. The magnitude is the same whether an edge goes from dark to bright or from bright to dark, which is exactly the invariance the robe needs. The feature is recomputed at every pyramid level (one small convolution per level) and NCC is then computed on the feature maps exactly as before.

Results. emir aligns correctly (R = (40, 107), G = (23, 49)) and so does every other plate; L2 on gradient features also recovers church. Gradient features cost about 2.7 s per plate instead of 1.5 s, mostly for the percentile computation. The table compares raw-pixel and gradient NCC on all 18 plates; apart from emir they agree to within two pixels, and all further bells and whistles use gradient NCC as the alignment method.

7.2 Automatic cropping

Motivation. Aligned images have three kinds of bad border: the white scanner margin and the black film edge of each plate, the strips that np.roll wraps around from the opposite side, and the one-sided fringes where a shifted plate no longer covers the frame. Rather than cutting a fixed margin, I detect them.

Method. Three estimates are computed per side, combined by taking the maximum, and capped at 12 % of the dimension:

  1. Scan borders and frame separators on each raw plate. Looking at the rows (or columns) within 12 % of an edge, a row is "border-like" if its median is below 0.20 — the black film edge, or the dark separator line between two exposures; these bands are grainy and slightly tilted, so no flatness is required — or if at least 80 % of its pixels are above 0.88 and 95 % lie within 0.015 of the row median (the saturated, flat scanner margin; a bright sky is rarely that flat — the cloudless sky of ilemselga is the one exception in this set). Only runs of at least 6 consecutive border-like rows count. Everything from the edge to the inner end of the innermost such band is discarded, plus an 8 px pad. The "innermost" rule matters: the scan is cut into equal thirds, but the physical frame boundary lies up to ~130 px inside a third, and the rows between the cut and the separator show the neighbouring exposure — on church the bottom 100 rows of the blue plate are the top of the green exposure, which produced a cyan band along the bottom of every earlier version of that image.
  2. Verification against the aligned image. A dark band is ambiguous: a shadowed studio (isfandiyar), dark foliage (siren) or the shaded stall of melons are all darker than 0.20 in one plate. Real scene content, however dark, agrees with the other two aligned plates, while a margin, a separator or a foreign frame does not. So each dark band is checked in the aligned composite: a row counts as consistent if its texture (a 7 px running mean to suppress film grain, minus a 31 px running mean so slow illumination gradients do not count) correlates with another channel's (r > 0.4), or if its mean level, in units of each channel's own standard deviation, matches another channel's (|Δz| < 0.6). If 60 % of the band's rows are consistent it is scene content and the band is kept. Film grain is what makes the smoothing necessary: it is independent in every plate, so raw high-pass profiles of a dark background never correlate.
  3. Shift strips. Rolling a plate by (dx, dy) moves its own border by the same amount and wraps the leaving band to the other side; both are known exactly. In the reference frame the invalid band on top is bordertop + dy and at the bottom borderbottom − dy (likewise for x), maximised over the three channels.
  4. Channel disagreement (a safety net). Where the picture is good, the edges of the three aligned channels coincide, whatever their colours; along a bad border they do not. Each channel is z-scored, its Sobel magnitude computed, and the mean pairwise difference between the three magnitude maps is taken; rows and columns whose median disagreement exceeds the interior's median by 4 MAD (and 1.5×) are trimmed, but only the band that starts at the edge (an isolated disagreeing row deep inside the picture is content, not border). My first version compared z-scored intensities instead of gradients and trimmed every coloured sky to the cap — a colourful region disagrees in intensity even when it is perfectly aligned.

Finally 0.4 % of each dimension (about 13 px) is added on every side, because a thin fringe of blur and misregistration hugs every edge. The detector went through several iterations, each driven by looking at the results: fixed 0.10 darkness thresholds missed dark-gray film edges at 0.11–0.13; requiring dark bands to be flat missed the grainy separators; accepting every dark band cropped 20 % off the dark isfandiyar scene. The verification step is what reconciles the two failure modes.

Limitations. The disagreement safety net trims regions that genuinely differ between exposures — the wind-blown grass at the bottom of karlinskii costs 12 % of that image's height — and the perfectly uniform sky of ilemselga passes the white-margin test in the blue plate and costs 12 % of the top. The 12 % cap bounds every stage, so a border wider than that would survive. Everything is decided by fixed thresholds shared by all plates; nothing is tuned per image.

7.3 Automatic contrast

Method. A joint linear stretch: the 0.5th percentile of all channel values is mapped to 0 and the 99.5th to 1, clipping the rest. Using the same two numbers for all three channels keeps the channel ratios, and therefore the colours, unchanged — stretching each channel separately would be a hidden white balance. The stretch has to run after cropping: the saturated margins would otherwise pin both percentiles at 0 and 1 and nothing would happen. I also tried CLAHE (scikit-image's equalize_adapthist); it produces dramatic but unnatural, haloed results on these scans, so the percentile stretch is the default.

7.4 Automatic white balance

Method. White balance has two parts: estimating the colour of the illuminant, and dividing it out with per-channel gains (a von Kries diagonal transform). I implemented three classical estimators of the illuminant, all computed on the cropped image and ignoring pixels that are clipped in any channel:

  • gray world — the mean of each channel;
  • white patch — the 99th percentile of each channel (the brightest things should be white);
  • shades of gray — the Minkowski mean (mean of I6)1/6, which sits between the two and is the default.

Green is the anchor (gain 1) and the red and blue gains are clamped to [1/1.4, 1.4], so that a scene that really is colourful (the blue evening light on church, the melons) is not forced to gray. The figure shows the two plates with the strongest estimated casts, church and ilemselga, with all three estimators; the table lists the gains for every plate. Gray world over-corrects dominantly coloured scenes and white patch locks onto a blown-out sky, which is why shades of gray is the compromise I kept.

7.5 Better colour mapping: an experiment against the LoC composites

Motivation. There is no reason to assume that Prokudin-Gorskii's three filters correspond exactly to the R, G and B primaries of a display. The Library of Congress publishes a professionally produced colour composite of every plate (made for the Library by Blaise Agüera y Arcas in 2004), so instead of guessing a mapping I tried to measure one.

Method. For 6 plates with a known composite I (1) registered my aligned image to the LoC image with a translation + scale search using gradient NCC at 400 px working resolution (the LoC frame is cropped differently, so the scale is about 0.86), (2) sampled corresponding colours (both images box-blurred, every fourth pixel of the central region, saturated pixels excluded — about 5000 pairs per plate), and (3) solved T ≈ A Mᵀ + b for a 3 × 3 matrix and offset by least squares with the 20 % worst residuals trimmed. To decide whether such a matrix is real, I validated it leave-one-plate-out against two baselines: the identity, and a per-channel gain + offset (a "diagonal" matrix, i.e. what white balance and contrast already do). The matrix would only be adopted if it beat the identity by 25 % and the diagonal fit by 10 % on the held-out plates and its off-diagonal terms were consistent across plates.

7.6 Better transformations: rotation and scale

Rotation search. I extended the coarse-to-fine search with a rotation dimension: at the coarsest level the moving plate is rotated by every angle in [−2°, 2°] in 0.25° steps (17 angles, each with the full translation search), the best angle is refined to ±0.125° at the next level, and the finer levels search translation only. At full resolution the translation-only hypothesis (0°) is scored as well and kept if it is at least as good — rotation is only adopted when it genuinely helps. The rotation itself is the one place where I use a library resampling routine (bilinear skimage.transform.rotate).

Two honest checks. First, a synthetic one: I rotated the B channel of two real plates by 1.5° and shifted it by (4, −6). Translation-only search cannot fit this and leaves the corners misaligned; the rotation search recovers −1.5° and (−4, 6) exactly:

Second, the real plates. Every fitted angle is within ±0.375°, i.e. within the resolution of the search — the plates are, to a very good approximation, pure translations, as one would expect from a rigid plate holder. (A bias of one step towards non-zero angles is expected: bilinear resampling slightly smooths the image, which by itself raises the NCC of gradient features.)

Opposite corners moving in opposite directions along the diagonals is the signature of a scale difference, not a rotation (a rotation would move the corners tangentially). A plausible physical explanation is that the three exposures were focused slightly differently — the lens has a different focal length for blue and red light — so one plate is imaged a fraction of a percent larger than another. This also explains why the best integer translation depends on the metric: with a scale error, no single translation is right everywhere, and the fringes I could still see in the corners of three_generations after translation-only alignment are exactly this effect.

Similarity refinement. Searching over scale exhaustively at full resolution is expensive, but it is unnecessary. The residual shifts of a 3 × 3 grid of windows are a direct measurement of the transform: for a similarity transform about the image centre the residual at window centre p is (A − I) p + t with A = s R(θ), which is linear in (s cos θ − 1, s sin θ, tx, ty). I solve it by least squares over the windows (windows with a weak NCC peak or a shift on the search boundary are discarded, and windows that disagree with the fitted model by more than 2 px are dropped and the fit repeated), warp the plate once with the fitted scale, rotation and translation, and re-measure the window residuals.

7.7 Another data source: photographic plates of the Palomar sky survey

Astronomical imaging still works like Prokudin-Gorskii's camera: one filter at a time. The Second Palomar Observatory Sky Survey (POSS-II) photographed the sky on glass plates through blue (J), red (F) and near-infrared (N) emulsion/filter combinations on different nights, and the Space Telescope Science Institute serves scans of them as FITS files. I fetched a 30′ field around the Dumbbell Nebula (M27) from the three surveys, deliberately requesting each plate with a slightly different field centre (offsets of 30–40″) so that the cutouts are not pre-registered by the archive's plate solutions. A small FITS reader (2880-byte header blocks of 80-character cards, big-endian 16-bit data scaled by BSCALE/BZERO) turns them into arrays; each plate is sky-subtracted, scaled by its 99.7th percentile and given an arcsinh stretch, the standard way to show stars without saturating everything. Then the same rigid search as in 7.6 (gradient NCC) aligns the red and infrared plates to the blue one, and the shades-of-gray white balance is applied. The result is a "false colour" image: infrared → R, red → G, blue → B.

The three exposures span six years and different emulsions, so star brightnesses differ a lot between plates — the same kind of contrast mismatch as emir — and gradient NCC handles it. As an independent check I detect stars in the blue plate and in each aligned plate (bright compact blobs above the 99.5th percentile, intensity-weighted centroids) and match them within 3 px: the median offset of the matched stars after alignment is a fraction of a pixel. Individual stars still show small colour halos because the plates have different point-spread functions, which no alignment can remove. Some stars are genuinely red or blue: that is real astrophysics, not misregistration.

8. Reproducibility and runtime

Everything is in the code/ directory of the submission (Python 3.12, NumPy, SciPy, scikit-image for reading/rotating, Pillow for I/O, Matplotlib for the histograms). Alignment, the metrics and the pyramid are implemented from scratch in align.py, metrics.py and pyramid.py; main.py is the command line entry point and every number on this page is rendered from the results.json it writes. The full run is:

cd code && uv sync                  # or: pip install -r requirements.txt
